Section 2: Learning Intentions

From the syllabus to students' own words.

A learning intention names what students will learn. It starts in the syllabus and is written in words students understand.

Key ideas

From syllabus to student voice

1Outcome

MA2-MR-01 represents and uses the structure of multiplicative relations to 10 × 10 to solve problems

2Content

Use arrays to establish multiplication facts. Use number properties to find related multiplication facts.

3Student-friendly learning intention

We are learning to use arrays to find all the ways to multiply to make a number.

Finding the right size

Too broad

We are learning about area.

Names the topic, not what students will do.
Too task-specific

We are learning to design a garden bed.

Students remember the garden, not the maths.
Just right

We are learning to compare the areas of rectangles that have the same perimeter.

Specific and transferable. MA3-2DS-02

Try it: learning, activity or context?

Read more: making it student friendly
  • Begin with "We are learning to…" and one action verb students can picture: identify, show, explain, compare, create.
  • Keep one idea per intention. Split a long content point across lessons.
  • Swap syllabus phrasing for everyday words, but keep the key subject term (array, paragraph, habitat) and teach it.
  • Test it: can a student say it back in their own words?
  • K–2: short enough to say in one breath, paired with a picture or symbol, said aloud and echoed with a partner.
Read more: what a learning intention isn't
  • Vague: "Today we are learning about fractions."
  • Written for teachers: "Students will understand the main idea and supporting details of a text."
  • Copied from the syllabus without breaking it down or unpacking the terms.
  • Copied into books word for word. Copying is not understanding.
  • Displayed at the start and never mentioned again.
  • A different intention for individual students or fixed groups.

Section 3: The Success Criteria Ladder

Three to five criteria that move from a low floor start to transfer.

Three to five success criteria that give every student a starting point and a way to go further. Every student works up the same ladder.

Key ideas
1Start

A low floor starting point every student can reach, with support if needed.

name, identify, tell, build, sort
2Demonstrate

Show the knowledge and skills being learned.

show, explain, measure, draw, write
3Apply

Use the knowledge and skills in a familiar situation, like the one modelled.

use, solve, choose, plan, create
4Transfer

Apply them in an unfamiliar situation: transfer or generalise.

adapt, generalise, predict, justify
Low floor: everyone can startHigh ceiling: transfer
Three to five criteria Three: combine the starting point and demonstrating into one criterion, for example in a short or early-years lesson. Five: add a second Demonstrate or Apply criterion when the skill has distinct steps. Whatever the number, all four opportunities are there.
Value equally knowledge, skills, mastery, and application of learning.FAITH, LEARNING & WELLBEING FRAMEWORK · RIGOUR

On the board

Global learning intentionTo represent and interpret data
We are learning toRepresent data on a column graph
To be successful, we will
  1. StartIdentify the parts of a column graph: title, axes, labels and scale
  2. DemonstrateDraw a column graph of our class data using an even scale
  3. ApplyChoose a suitable scale and graph a new set of class data
  4. TransferUse a graph to answer a question about data from somewhere new, like a news report, and explain my thinking
Read more: writing criteria well
  • Write the Apply criterion first. What does using this learning in a familiar situation look like at stage level? Then work down to the start and up to transfer.
  • Make the start a genuine low floor. Every student should be able to reach it, with support if needed. State it as what students can do, not what is missing.
  • Make transfer genuinely new. A different context, audience, number or problem, not more of the same.
  • Describe what students do, say or make. Leave out words like good, neat, clear, detailed.
  • Decontextualise. "I can include arguments for and against" transfers. "I can explain why uniforms are popular" doesn't.
  • Change the thinking, not the quantity. "3 sentences / 5 sentences / 8 sentences" is not a ladder.
  • Name the language demands of the task, so EAL/D learners know what language to use.
  • Students working well above or below stage level, including Life Skills, work from the matching syllabus outcomes, on the same or a similar task where possible.

Section 4: Sharing and Using Them in the Lesson

Making learning intentions and success criteria work from launch to close.

Share them early. Check students understand them. Come back to them all lesson.

Key ideas

Across the lesson

BeforePlan
  • Intention and criteria from the syllabus
  • A strong example and a non-example
  • Vocabulary to teach and one check for understanding
LaunchShare and clarify
  • Explain in student-friendly words
  • Connect to what students already know
  • Compare the example and non-example
  • Students say it in their own words
DuringRefer back
  • Questions tied to the criteria
  • Feedback using the criteria
  • Self and peer feedback
CloseSummarise
  • Restate what we learned
  • Students find their place on the ladder and set a next step
  • Link to next lesson

A feedback routine

I am looking for…you to find every factor pair of 24 and show how you know none are missing.
You have…found four pairs and drawn an array for each.
You could…list your pairs in order from 1 to check for gaps.

Sentence stems for self and peer feedback

  • I have shown… by…
  • I am on step… of the ladder because…
  • You have… You could try… next.
  • My next step is… I'll know I've done it when…
Read more: is it working? A student-voice check

At the end of a lesson, ask five students two questions and score each answer from 0 to 4. Repeat in a few weeks. This gives you evidence for Monitor & collect evidence and Reflect & evaluate.

Read more: whole-school routines and families

When every class shares and uses learning intentions the same way, students learn the routine once and spend their attention on the learning. Agree on a routine as a staff, then plan and rehearse it together before taking it into classrooms. Section 8 has rehearsal prompts.

Quality check

Read more: rehearse with a colleague

Teachers are more likely to use new practice when they plan and rehearse it first, without students, and get feedback.

Steps

  1. Build your intention and ladder from the syllabus.
  2. Plan how you'll introduce them, the vocabulary to teach and your check for understanding.
  3. Rehearse the launch with a partner.
  4. Refine using their feedback, then swap.

Feedback prompts

  • Was the intention from the syllabus and in student-friendly language?
  • Did the criteria build to application?
  • Was the link to prior learning made?
  • Was new vocabulary taught?
  • Was understanding checked before students started?
